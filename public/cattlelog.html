Cattlelog: Surfacing grade data on a course page used by 5,800+ students monthly

TLDR

Cattlelog is a UC Davis course-planning tool with 5,800+ monthly users, but to see a course’s grades, students had to leave its page and open a separate Grades tab, and only 41.5% ever did. My co-designer and I put grade charts right on the course page, with a side-by-side professor comparison. To keep the page from getting cluttered, we put the full breakdown behind a “View more” link, and we kept the old Grades tab, since 386 students still used it. In testing, all 8 students found grades without help.

Overview

Grades were just one click away, but half of students never clicked

Cattlelog is a UC Davis course-planning tool with 5,800+ monthly users. Its course page showed almost everything students needed to choose a class, except grades, which sat behind a button on a separate page. Only 41.5% of course page visitors clicked through.

My co-designer and I brought grade distributions onto the course page, and all 8 students in testing found them without help.

Problem

Finding grades took more clicks than it should

The course page was too narrow for charts, so grades got scattered across three features: the course page, a Grade Hub, and a Compare tool. Most students only found them through one button.

Solution

Everything about a course, on one page

Grades up front

Grade distributions and GPA trends now load right next to the course details, so students see them without clicking anything.

Compare professors side by side

Students pick the professors teaching the course, and each one shows up on the same chart in their own color, so it’s easy to spot who grades harder.

A course page with room for charts

The old course page was a narrow column, so the grade chart lived on a separate page behind a button. Giving each course its own full page made room to put the chart right on it.

The full breakdown is still there

Students who want more can open “View more” to see grades by professor and quarter, without crowding the course page.

Research

Students already knew where to look

My first instinct was to figure out how students were actually getting to grades. I dug into 90 days of PostHog data, then ran usability tests where students talked through their thinking out loud.

Process

Fixing the path students already use

Since the course page was already where students went for grades, that’s where I focused. I worked on the course catalog and course pages; the Grade Hub, Compare, and the rest of Cattlelog stayed as they were.

Weighing our options

OptionProsCons
Make the button biggerQuick to buildWe tried it, and it barely moved the numbers. Students still had to leave the course page to see grades.
Improve the Grade HubAlready builtNone of our testers found it, and it stayed empty until you picked courses. It’s just not where students start.
Put grades on the course page Grades show up where students already lookIt meant a bigger redesign, since the course page needed room for charts.

Putting grades right on the course page was the only option that got rid of the click entirely.

Making room for grades

The real problem was space. The old course page was a narrow column with no room for charts, so we gave courses their own page with grades right next to the details. I also added a professor picker, so you can compare professors side by side, each in their own colour.

Tradeoffs we made

The full breakdown still opens on its own page
Fitting every chart on the course page would’ve made it long and cluttered. I was worried leaving the page would feel annoying, but Berkeleytime and Bruinwalk work the same way, so students already know the pattern. Testers clicked through to it without any prompting.
We kept the Grades tab
We thought about removing it, since the course page drove 9× more grade traffic. But 386 students still used it to browse grades across courses, and a nav link costs almost nothing. So we made the course page the main path and kept the tab. After launch, we’ll check whether people still use it.

Don’t forget empty searches

Students ran over 150,000 searches in 90 days, and a search with no matches just said “No results found.” I added an empty state that explains what happened and gives students a way back to the course list.

Testing

The first version held up

Instead of static mockups, I built a working prototype with Claude Code so 8 students could click through real grade data. With grades on the course page, everyone saw them right away, so the real question was the tradeoff:

Does leaving the page for the full breakdown feel annoying?
No one brought it up, and testers clicked through without any prompting.

Since testing went smoothly, the design went straight into development.

Reflection

What I learned

Fix the path people already use
It’s tempting to make something more visible by adding more ways to get there. But students had already picked their route. The better move was making that route do more.
Look for the root cause, not the symptom
Three grade features looked like the problem, but the real issue was a course page with no room. Fixing the space fixed the reason the features kept multiplying.
Prototype with real data
Building in code meant testers reacted to real grade distributions, so their feedback matched how they’d actually use the page.
