Overview

Grades were one click away, and half of students never clicked

Cattlelog is a UC Davis course-planning tool with 5,800+ monthly users. Its course page showed almost everything students needed to choose a class, except grades, which sat behind a button on a separate page. Only 41.5% of course page visitors clicked through.

My co-designer and I brought grade distributions onto the course page, and all 8 students in testing found them without help.

Solution

Everything about a course, on one page

Grade distributions and GPA trends now sit right next to the course details. No extra click needed.

Problem

Finding grades took more clicks than it should

UC Davis students usually plan classes with two tools that don’t talk to each other. Schedule Builder has every class but no professor ratings. Rate My Professors has ratings but is missing a lot of classes. Cattlelog brings both together.

Over two years, though, grade data spread across three features: course grade pages, a Grade Hub, and a Compare tool. All three pulled from the same database, but each had its own flow, its own filters, and even its own chart. Compare, for example, dropped +/− grades entirely.

The root cause was space. The course page lived in a narrow third column with no room for charts, so grade data kept getting pushed into new features. And students only really used one of them: the course page’s “View Grade Distributions” button.

Research

Students already knew where to look

My first instinct was to figure out how students were actually getting to grades. I dug into 90 days of PostHog data, then ran usability tests where students talked through their thinking out loud.

Testing backed this up. When I asked students to find grades, none of the testers found the Grade Hub or Compare. The Hub had another issue too: it opened empty until you picked courses, so students who landed there didn’t know what to do. And when students wanted to compare several classes, they skipped Compare and just opened more browser tabs.

So students had already picked their path to grades. The problem was that it ended at a button.

Process

Fixing the path students already use

Since the course page was already where students went for grades, that’s where I focused. I worked on the course catalog and course pages; the Grade Hub, Compare, and the rest of Cattlelog stayed as they were.

Putting grades right on the course page was the only option that got rid of the click entirely.

Making room for grades

The real constraint was space. In the old three-column layout, the course page had no room for charts, which is why grade data kept spilling into other features. So we gave courses their own page, with details on the left and grades on the right.

I also added a professor selector, so students can compare professors for the same course in one view. Each professor gets a color, and their grades overlay on both the grade distribution and GPA trend charts.

Tradeoffs we made

The full breakdown still opens on its own page
Fitting every chart on the course page would’ve made it long and cluttered. I was worried leaving the page would feel annoying, but Berkeleytime and Bruinwalk work the same way, so students already know the pattern. In testing, nobody even hesitated.
We kept the Grades tab
We thought about removing it, since the course page drove 9× more grade traffic. But 386 students still used it to browse grades across courses, and a nav link costs almost nothing. So we made the course page the main path and kept the tab. After launch, we’ll check whether people still use it.

Don’t forget empty searches

The Grade Hub showed me how much an empty screen can lose people, and search had the same risk. Students search Cattlelog a lot, with over 150,000 searches in 90 days. When a search came up empty, they’d just see “No results found.” I suggested a blank state with the Cattlelog logo and a short message, like “No courses found, please check your search,” so students know what happened and what to try next.

Testing

The first version held up

Instead of static mockups, I built a working prototype with Claude Code so students could click through real grade data. I watched for two things:

Do students notice grades without being told?
All 8 did.
Does leaving the page for the full breakdown feel annoying?
Nobody hesitated or brought it up.

Since testing went smoothly, the design went straight into development.

Reflection

What I learned

Fix the path people already use
It’s tempting to make something more visible by adding more ways to get there. But students had already picked their route. The better move was making that route do more.
Look for the root cause, not the symptom
Three grade features looked like the problem, but the real issue was a course page with no room. Fixing the space fixed the reason the features kept multiplying.
Prototype with real data
Building in code meant testers reacted to real grade distributions, so their feedback matched how they’d actually use the page.
